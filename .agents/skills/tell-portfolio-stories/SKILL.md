# Tell portfolio stories

Create a story a reader can understand without knowing the project. Borrow scene-led pacing from Growth.Design, especially the Apple sleep-notification reference, while preserving Dhwani's own voice, fonts, colors and component language.

Reference: https://growth.design/case-studies/apple-sleep-notification
Treat the reference as a storytelling example, not project evidence or a design system. Browse it when a new detailed comparison is requested. Do not copy its text, characters, illustrations or fonts.

## Establish evidence before writing

1. Read the current project brief, checklist and original artifacts relevant to the requested case. Use available Drive, Figma and repository access within authorized scope. Follow connector skills before calling their tools.
2. Build a private claim ledger with columns: claim; exact source location; source date/version; personal versus team ownership; status; public clearance.
3. Use these statuses:
   - **Verified:** directly supported by an inspected original artifact.
   - **User-confirmed:** explicitly supplied or corrected by Dhwani; identify this basis in the ledger.
   - **Interpretation:** a design rationale or behavioral reading, clearly separated from observed findings.
   - **Unresolved:** missing evidence, inaccessible artifact, conflicting versions, or uncertain scope.
4. Treat current user corrections as authoritative for intended content. Reconcile factual conflicts instead of silently combining sources. Keep conflicting counts, dates and outcomes out of public copy until resolved.
5. Treat existing portfolio text, repository narratives, assistant drafts and memory as leads to investigate, not independent verification. Never use an earlier assistant answer to corroborate another.
6. Do not claim to have inspected a linked report, prototype or screen unless it was actually opened. Record access failures. Continue with evidence that is available.
7. Require support for every number, quotation, timeline, research finding, implementation claim and outcome. Do not turn a recommendation into a shipped feature, positive feedback into adoption, or a prototype into demonstrated impact.
8. Distinguish what Dhwani did, what collaborators did, and what the team delivered. Keep ownership visible in the opening.
9. Keep Prime Video confidential. Retain an NDA-only treatment unless Dhwani provides explicit clearance for specific material. Generalize sensitive operational examples; use clearly fictional records only where permitted. Never publish private source documents or internal details merely because they are accessible.
10. If evidence is thin, write a shorter truthful story. Ask only focused questions that affect a central claim when interaction is possible; otherwise leave a precise gap in the ledger.

Do not announce that all content is accurate after a partial audit. State which claims were checked and which remain unresolved.

## Choose one lane

Write a one-sentence spine before drafting:

**Someone needed to [task], but [observed friction]. [Evidence] changed my understanding, so I [personal decision]. We delivered [supported result]; [limit] remains.**

Use this as a scaffold, not public boilerplate. Choose the decision that best demonstrates Dhwani's judgment. Keep secondary projects, every method used, and unrelated wins out of the central story.

For a portfolio case, lead with a task and decision, rather than critiquing a company's product as if this were an independent teardown.

## Build the story in this order

| Beat | Reader's question | What to show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task | Who is trying to do what? | One observed task or a labeled illustrative scenario |
| Friction | Where does it get difficult? | Relevant screen, workflow step or research artifact, with one annotation |
| Finding | What changed your understanding? | The specific observation, source excerpt or synthesis that prompted a change |
| Decision | What did you choose, and why? | Dhwani's action, a credible alternative and the real trade-off |
| Changed experience | What does the direction let someone do? | Matched before/after task views or a labeled proposal |
| Reasoning | Why does this design choice fit the evidence? | A short explanation tied to the actual interaction |
| Result and limit | What happened, and what is still unknown? | Delivered/tested/shipped status, supported outcome and next question |

Combine or omit beats when evidence or scope warrants it. Do not force seven equally sized paragraphs, seven clicks or the same lesson onto every project. Make section headlines advance the story: a doubt, discovery or decision.

## Make it understandable in 30 seconds

At the top, provide:
- A concrete headline naming the tension or change.
- Three short beats: problem → finding → my decision.
- Personal role and supported result.
- One decisive visual with a plain caption.
- Approximate reading time, derived from visible prose rather than a fixed promise.

Aim for roughly 150–250 words in the core story when appropriate; use optional details for depth. A deeper read may take two or three minutes, but do not remove important constraints to meet an arbitrary word count.

Keep normal scrolling as the default. Offer slides only as an optional reading mode. Never require an interaction to see the main argument.

Use everyday language and first person only where ownership is supported. Prefer specific verbs such as observed, mapped, challenged, prototyped and recommended. Remove generic claims about passion, empathy, innovation and transformation. Read the draft aloud; replace anything Dhwani would not naturally say.

## Give the visuals a job

For each visual, define:
1. The question it answers.
2. The artifact and its provenance.
3. The one detail to highlight.
4. A caption explaining why that detail mattered.

Use original cleared screens first. Keep the task, scale and crop comparable across before/after images. Provide access to full-size media. Avoid unreadable full-canvas screenshots.

When media is absent, use a clearly labeled screen placeholder or schematic. Never generate a fake project screenshot, research note, participant quotation, metric or client artifact.

Use phone shells for mobile designs, wide frames for desktop designs, and appropriate dashboard frames for in-car concepts. Do not imply a design was tested on a particular device because it appears in a mockup. Preserve screen aspect ratio and legibility.

Inherit the portfolio's typography and theme tokens. Growth.Design informs story sequence and reveal timing, not font choice. Use Dhwani's actual supplied Apple Memoji if available; otherwise keep an explicit avatar slot rather than inventing her likeness.

## Use dialogue responsibly

Use brief authored commentary at a doubt, discovery or trade-off:
- "I initially thought..." only when that initial view is supported.
- "This finding changed..." only when the source supports that change.
- "I chose..." only when the personal decision is confirmed.

Label commentary as authored narration. Label fictional task prompts as illustrative scenarios. Quote participants only with verified wording and appropriate clearance. Never invent thoughts, emotions, dialogue, motivations or stakes.

Let readers skip the guide without losing evidence. Keep dialogue concise and avoid making the character repeat the adjacent paragraph.

## Explain psychology without overstating it

Start from an observed behavior and a concrete design choice. Add a psychology or HCI principle only if it clarifies that connection.

Verify the principle in a primary paper or authoritative HCI source and link it. Frame the explanation as an interpretation unless this project actually measured the effect. Do not diagnose participants, claim bias from a design preference, or use a principle as proof of success.

If the principle adds jargon but no understanding, omit it.

## Make interactions carry the story

Add an interaction only when its purpose is clear:
- A before/after toggle compares the same task.
- A highlight isolates the decisive screen detail.
- Problem → Finding → Decision brings relevant evidence forward.
- A connected thread explains why one finding led to a decision.
- Plan versus learning exposes a documented revision.
- A sticky note opens concise rationale.
- A restrained cursor spotlight draws attention inside a visual frame.
- A compact chapter rail follows scrolling and exposes section names.

Use native controls, visible focus, touch alternatives and reduced-motion behavior. Keep decorative layers from blocking input. Show important content without hover or JavaScript. Avoid per-pointer-move React state and unnecessary global listeners.

Do not add endless interactions as a substitute for real screens or a clear story. Keep the layout spacious and make one detail the focus at a time.

## Prepare whiteboard presentations

Expand the same verified spine rather than writing a new, inconsistent account. Use one argument per frame: context, task, friction, evidence, turning point, alternatives, decision, prototype, evaluation, contribution, outcome and open question.

Adapt the number of frames to evidence. Put detailed data and citations beside the relevant argument or in an appendix. Keep the claim ledger linked to each frame. Do not fill slides with invented data to create apparent depth.

## Finish with a reviewable result

Before delivering:
- Trace each factual sentence to the ledger; remove unresolved public claims.
- Check that a new reader can identify the task, friction, personal choice and supported outcome from the opening.
- Check that visuals demonstrate the central decision and that all missing media is labeled.
- Check that personal contribution and team results are not conflated.
- Check that outcome limits and confidentiality remain explicit.
- For code changes, follow repository instructions, run relevant checks, verify the available preview, and update the project checklist.
- Report exactly what was verified; do not call mobile, motion or browser checks complete unless exercised.
- For writing-only tasks, deliver the story plus a concise source/gap ledger. Do not deploy unrelated site changes merely because this skill was invoked.

## Evidence checks to rehearse

- If a public page says four tabs and earlier notes say five, omit the count and log the conflict.
- If a prototype exists but no launch evidence does, describe the prototype rather than adoption.
- If interview findings are real but a quote is unavailable, paraphrase with provenance rather than fabricate dialogue.
- If the team synthesized research, name Dhwani's confirmed contribution rather than claiming all research as hers.
- If a principle seems plausible but is unsourced, explain the design mechanism plainly and leave the principle out.
